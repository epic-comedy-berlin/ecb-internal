Using Slack

How ECB uses Slack, what each channel is for, and how to set things up so you stay in the loop without being constantly interrupted.

The short version: Slack is how the team communicates. It's where you flag things, share things, and talk to each other between meetings.
← Back to all guides

What Slack is for

Slack is ECB's communication layer — the place where the team stays in sync between meetings. You use it to flag something that needs attention, share an update, ask a quick question, or tell someone something needs doing.

Think of it as where the team talks. The actual output — strategy, drafts, decisions, builds — gets produced in meetings, in Claude, and in the tools the team uses day to day. Slack is the layer that keeps everyone connected to all of it.

After-hours messages

If a message is sent after 10pm, assume it won't be read until the next day. If something is urgent and nobody is responding, send a message in the WhatsApp group letting the team know to check Slack. Slack is not monitored around the clock — WhatsApp is the escalation path for anything that genuinely can't wait.

How Slack connects to Claude

Several channels feed directly into the AI system. What gets posted in them isn't just communication — it's part of the record Claude uses to stay up to date.

The meeting channels. Before every meeting, the agenda is posted in #meeting-agendas — the meeting lead uses it to run the session, and everyone should read it before joining. After every meeting, a Meeting Intelligence Report is posted in #mir-reports — a structured summary of what was discussed, decided, and actioned. These reports are one of the primary sources Claude uses to update the project files. See the Meeting Agendas guide and MIR guide for the full process.

The log update scan. When Claude runs a log update — the process that keeps the task files accurate — it scans all Slack channels as source material, with different priority levels per channel. #p0-tasks and #p1-important are scanned at the highest priority. #p2-general is checked for anything misposted. #mir-reports and #p4-claude-info are used as reference. This means what gets posted anywhere in Slack can feed into the project files — which is why posting in the right channel matters.

The Claude output channel. When Claude produces long research or strategic analysis, the output gets posted in #p4-claude-info for the team to read. Reactions and discussion should stay as a thread reply on that specific message rather than being posted as a new message elsewhere. Any decisions that come out of those discussions get captured in the next log update.

How Slack actually works

Most people start using Slack like a group chat — posting messages into a channel and replying in the same stream. That works for a while, but it gets hard to follow quickly. Here's how to use it in a way that keeps things readable.

Set up your profile
Do this first

Before anything else, set up your profile so the team and the AI system know who's who. Click your avatar in the top right → Profile → Edit.

Set three things: a profile photo (a clear photo of your face, not a logo or avatar), your real name as the display name, and your role in the title field — e.g. "Production & Tech", "Strategy", "Marketing & Host".

This matters more than it looks. Consistent real names mean messages, mentions, and meeting transcripts all attribute correctly — when Claude processes a transcript or scans Slack, it needs to know who said what. A photo makes channels easier to scan at a glance.

Messages and threads
The most important thing to understand

Every message in a channel can start a thread. When you want to reply to a specific message — rather than post a new standalone message — hover over it and click Reply in thread. Your reply gets attached to that message and keeps the main channel clean.

Think of the channel as the noticeboard and threads as the conversations that branch off each post. Without threads, a channel with lots of replies becomes one long stream that's hard to follow. With threads, each conversation stays grouped together.

Post in the channel when sharing something new everyone should see.
Reply in thread when responding to something specific already posted. In Slack, "Reply in thread" and "Reply" mean the same thing — both open the thread attached to that message. The important thing is using it rather than posting a fresh message in the channel.

You can also format messages for clarity — *asterisks* make text bold, _underscores_ make it italic, and you can add bullet points or numbered lists. Useful when a message has a few distinct points rather than one line.

O
Ori 11:42 AM
Just confirmed the Zosch booking for next Friday — we're on.
👍 2 Reply in thread
2 replies in thread
B
Brendan 11:45 AM
What time does load-in start?
O
Ori 11:47 AM
7pm. Sound check at 7:30.

The main channel shows the original message. The follow-up questions stay in the thread — visible to anyone who needs them, out of the way for everyone else.

@ mentions
Getting someone's attention directly

Typing @name in a message sends that person a direct notification regardless of their channel settings. Use it when something specifically needs their attention rather than the whole team's.

@here notifies everyone currently active in the channel. @channel notifies all members whether they're online or not. Use both sparingly — they interrupt everyone. Reserve @channel for something genuinely urgent that the whole team needs to see immediately.

Reactions
Acknowledging without adding noise

Hover over any message and click the smiley face icon to add an emoji reaction. A 👍 or ✅ tells someone you've seen their message and you're across it — without creating a new notification or cluttering the channel with "ok", "got it", "sounds good" replies.

This is especially useful in #p0-tasks and #p1-important — a ✅ reaction on a task completion means "acknowledged" without any back-and-forth.

Sharing files
Clips, invoices, documents

To share a file — a clip, an invoice, a screenshot, a document — either drag it straight into the message box, or click the + icon to the left of where you type and choose Upload from your computer. Add a short note saying what it is before you send, so people know what they're looking at.

Post files in the channel they belong to — clips in #content, invoices in #finance, reports in #p4-claude-info. A file dropped in the right channel with a one-line description is easy to find later; one dropped in the wrong channel with no context gets lost.

Direct messages
Use with care

You can send a private message directly to another team member by clicking their name or using the New message button. DMs are useful for genuinely private conversations — personal matters, sensitive feedback, or something that only concerns one person.

For everything else, prefer public channels. Decisions or information shared in DMs disappear from the team record — Claude can't scan them, they don't feed into the log update, and the rest of the team has no visibility. If something is work-related, it almost always belongs in a channel.

Voice messages
Use sparingly

Slack lets you record and send voice notes using the microphone icon in the message bar. However, the ECB team is on the free plan which doesn't include transcription — meaning Claude can't read or process voice messages. Anything said in a voice note won't be picked up by the AI system.

As a team norm, default to text. Voice messages are only appropriate for quick, informal exchanges that don't need to be captured — or when tone of voice genuinely matters and a text message risks being misread. For anything that needs to be on record or actioned later, write it out.

Finding and fixing things
Search, edit, delete

Search. The search bar at the top of Slack finds any past message, link, or file across every channel you're in. Useful for digging up a decision someone made, a link that was shared, or a past conversation — far faster than scrolling. You can narrow a search to a specific channel or person using the filters that appear.

Editing and deleting. To fix a typo or remove something you posted by mistake, hover over your own message, click the three dots, and choose Edit message or Delete message. Editing keeps the channel accurate; deleting clears genuine mistakes so they don't confuse anyone later.

Setting up notifications

The default Slack notification settings ping you for every message in every channel. That's too much. Here's how to set it up so you only hear about things that actually need your attention.

How to get there

Click your avatar / name → Preferences → Notifications

On mobile: tap the You tab → Notifications. Desktop and mobile settings are separate — set both.

Recommended settings

Notify me about

Set to Direct messages, mentions & keywords. You'll only be notified when someone directly needs you — not every message in every channel.

Do Not Disturb

Turn this on and set your quiet hours. A good starting point is 10pm – 8am. Adjust to suit your schedule. Anything sent after 10pm can wait until morning.

Per-channel overrides

For channels you want to watch more closely, open the channel → click the channel name at the top → Notifications → set to All new messages for that channel only.

Muting channels

If a channel generates too much noise, mute it rather than leaving. Right-click the channel name → Mute channel. You'll still see messages when you open it — you just won't get notified.

Don't forget mobile

Desktop and mobile notification settings don't sync. If you only update one, your phone will still ping you when your laptop is silent. Set both to the same schedule.

Bold channels have unread messages. Red badges mean you've been directly mentioned — those need attention first.

The sidebar is alphabetical. When you open Slack, scan for bold channels and work through them by priority — p0 and p1 first, then the rest as time allows.

Channels with no bold text have nothing new — you don't need to open them.

To clear a channel without opening it, right-click the channel name → Mark as read.

Before every meeting

Check #meeting-agendas before joining any team session. The agenda is posted there beforehand and the meeting lead uses it to run the session. Reading it first means you're not catching up while the meeting is already in progress.

Questions?

Post in #p2-general or ask Claude directly — it knows the channel structure and can tell you where something belongs.
